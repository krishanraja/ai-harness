## The signal test

A question is worth asking only if its two most likely answers would lead to materially different work. Run every candidate through that test.

"What format do you want?" usually fails because the wrapper can be adjusted later. "What happens if we do nothing?" often passes because the answer can change whether the task is worth doing or how urgently it should be owned.

Rank candidate questions by expected route change, not by how conventional they sound. Ask the highest-signal remaining question and recalculate after every answer.

## Push past the obvious

Scope, format, deadline, and audience are logistics. Ask only when they are genuinely unknown, cannot be discovered, and change the route. They do not become high-signal merely because they are conventional briefing questions.

Look especially for:

- **What changed:** why this is live now rather than earlier.
- **What is downstream:** who experiences the result, what decision it feeds, and what happens next.
- **What has already been tried:** failed attempts often reveal the actual bar.
- **What would make this a waste:** the negative boundary can expose the success standard cleanly.
- **What is conspicuously specific or omitted:** unusual emphasis may point to the real constraint.

## Five intent territories

These are territories to cover across the interview, not a fixed list of questions.

### 1. The real goal

The stated goal is often an artifact. The real goal is what becomes possible, changes, or stops being Krish's burden after the artifact exists.

### 2. The why

Ask for Krish's motivation when it changes the work, not merely the generic business rationale. The two may diverge.

### 3. The constraints

Surface what cannot be done, must remain true, has already been decided, or would create unacceptable cost, risk, or relationship damage.

### 4. The clear win

Make success concrete enough that `verification-loop` can later test it. Ask what would make the result obviously useful and what would make it a waste.

### 5. What the win should feel like

Capture Krish's own words. This often carries the register, confidence, relief, ambition, or audience reaction that a mechanical success criterion misses.

## Follow-ups

A follow-up does not count against the five-question ceiling. Ask one when an answer reveals:

- **A gap:** an outcome without a mechanism, or a mechanism without an outcome.
- **A contradiction:** the answer conflicts with an earlier answer or known constraint. Name both sides plainly and ask which governs.
- **An opportunity:** the answer suggests a materially better version of the task. State the opportunity and ask whether it is in scope; never silently redesign the work.

Do not follow up on interesting detail that cannot change the execution, authority, or verification plan.

## Questions to reject

Never ask:

- anything discoverable through safe inspection;
- anything with a strong reversible default;
- permission disguised as a quality question;
- two questions in one sentence;
- a question whose answer will be ignored;
- for credentials, secret values, or sensitive detail that the task does not require;
- for abstract taste or working-style descriptions when `ctrl-intake` should elicit the durable standard from real artifacts.

## Refusal and low-engagement handling

"Use your judgment" is information: Krish prefers momentum over further alignment on this task.

Stop the interview. State the material assumptions and a compact plan. If the instruction clearly authorizes proceeding within the original scope, start after that summary unless a separate approval gate applies. Treat the first observable artifact or checkpoint as the next opportunity to validate the assumptions.
