## The AI Tell Taxonomy (Detection Reference)

The Hard Rules in the main skill ban specific offences. This section is the full catalogue of AI writing tropes, for two jobs: (1) scanning any draft before delivery, and (2) reviewing or de-AI-ing text Krish pastes in. If a pattern below appears and is not an intentional Krish signature move, rewrite it.

Important exception: the "Not X, Y" clarifier is a genuine Krish signature (see Signature Sentence Shapes in the main skill). It stays. The difference between his version and the AI version: his discards a specific lazy framing and replaces it with a sharper specific one. The AI version is empty contrast ("It's not about tools, it's about mindset"). Keep the former, kill the latter.

### Sentence-level constructions

- Tricolon addiction. Everything in threes ("faster, cheaper, smarter"). Two or four items is often more honest. Threes are permitted when the content genuinely has three parts, never as a rhythm default.
- Anaphora spam. Repeated sentence openers ("It means X. It means Y. It means Z.").
- The rhetorical question pivot. "The result?" "The catch?" "So what does this mean?" followed by a one-liner.
- Drama commands. "Let that sink in." "Read that again." "Sit with that."
- The single-sentence paragraph deployed for gravitas. One per piece, maximum, and only when earned.
- Perfectly parallel sentence pairs and clause mirroring.

### Word and phrase tells

This extends the Words to Avoid list in the main skill.

- Verb inflation: harness, unleash, elevate, supercharge, navigate, foster, cultivate.
- Adjective inflation: crucial, pivotal, vital, comprehensive, best-in-class.
- Testament ("a testament to"), boasts ("boasts 10,000 users"), underscores/highlights ("this underscores the importance of").
- Throat-clearing: "It's worth noting that", "It's important to remember", "Interestingly,".
- "Whether you're a X or a Y", the fake-inclusive audience frame.
- "Imagine a world where..." and "Did you know that 73%..." openers.

### Structural tells

- The hedge sandwich. "While X has benefits, it also has drawbacks. Ultimately it depends." A refusal to take a position wearing the costume of balance. One counterpoint per piece is intellectual honesty; symmetrical both-sidesing is cowardice.
- Symmetric paragraph lengths. Eerily uniform 3-4 sentence paragraphs throughout. Human writing has variance. Break the grid.
- Header-and-bullet everything, with bolded lead-ins on every bullet, where prose would flow better.
- Front-loaded definitions. Explaining a term to an audience that already knows it.
- The forced callback ending. Circling back to the opening metaphor with a bow on it.
- The call-to-action outro. "So next time you X, remember to Y."

### Tone tells

- The consultant voice. "Organisations must prioritise..." Agentless, passive prescriptions.
- Hedged certainty. "Can potentially help to possibly improve." Commit or cut.
- The empathy preamble. "We've all been there." "Sound familiar?"
- Empty superlatives: incredible, amazing, powerful, doing no work.
- Analogies that don't quite land. "Like a Swiss Army knife for your data." If the metaphor needs explaining, it failed. Krish's cross-metaphors (commercial for technical, technical for commercial) must map precisely or not appear.

### The meta-tell

Uniform confidence and uniform rhythm. AI output sands off variance: every claim equally hedged or equally confident, every sentence mid-length, every paragraph the same shape. Human writing, and Krish's specifically, has spikes: an unhedged strong claim next to a genuine "no one knows", a four-word sentence next to a forty-word one. If a draft reads smooth all the way through, it has failed even if no individual tell is present. This is the last check and the most important one, because it survives after every vocabulary tell has been stripped.

## Deck and Slide Copy Doctrine

Applies to any deck, talk, workshop, webinar, or slide artifact. Learned from a full rewrite of a Lightning Lesson deck that read AI-written despite passing the vocabulary kill list. Slides fail differently from prose, and these rules exist because the taxonomy alone did not catch it.

### Division of labour

The speaker carries the performance. Slides carry facts and numbers. Every slide-copy failure below is a version of the copy doing the speaker's job: commanding attention, narrating structure, reading the room, dropping the mic. Before writing a single slide, decide the delivery mode:

- **Solo mode.** Krish presents this himself, live. Authorised first-person field material stays first-person ("my current production fleet"), because it is the moat. Copy can run leaner because his delivery fills gaps.
- **Transferable mode.** The deck must survive a replay, a PDF leave-behind, or another presenter. First-person material becomes attributable evidence ("from a verified production fleet"). Every slide must be presentable by someone else reading and paraphrasing it. Retrieve any current count from the task's evidence packet rather than this doctrine.

Default to transferable unless told otherwise; most decks outlive the room they were built for.

### Zero-context legibility

A smart person with no prior exposure to the topic must be able to follow every slide cold.

- Define or replace jargon on first use, in the same breath, in one short clause ("a token, the small chunk of text AI is priced by"). If a term cannot be explained in one clause, cut the term and say the plain thing.
- One idea per slide. Two arguments on one slide means keep the stronger, relocate the other.
- Translate numbers into consequences. A percentage needs the plain sentence next to it saying what it does to someone's money, time, or decision.
- Headings state the point in plain words. Not clever, not coy, not functional filler.
- The reading test: could three different readers with three different backgrounds read the slide cold and correctly retell the point an hour later?

### Deck-scale kill list

These rules sit on top of the taxonomy and Hard Rules.

- **No commands to the reader.** "Read that again." "Look at the shape." "Hold onto this." "Notice." "Go and find them." "Say it out loud." Replace each with a plain statement of the fact the command pointed at. If the room needs to look again, the speaker says so, live, off-slide.
- **No deck self-narration.** No slide describes the deck's own structure, promises, or highlights. No "the spine of this hour", no "the slide you came for", no "why this slide is in the deck", no callbacks by slide number. Kicker labels are topic labels, never review blurbs.
- **No audience mind-reading.** Never predict what the room thinks, feels, or will do. Confidence about the material, zero omniscience about the reader.
- **Aphorism budget: six per deck.** Coined verdict lines land at act breaks and the final close only. Every other slide ends on a plain factual line. A deck that mic-drops on every slide is the uniform-rhythm meta-tell at deck scale; when everything is a verdict, nothing is.
- **"Not X, Y" budget: four per deck**, and only versions that discard a specific wrong framing for a specific sharper one. Empty contrasts die.
- **Analogies map exactly or don't appear.** No costume metaphors ("wearing X's clothing", "questions in a trench coat"). A good replacement explains the mechanism in ordinary words ("the bill moves with usage, like electricity, even though it arrives looking like rent").

### Structure rules

- Every slide answers one question a real audience member actually has. If the question cannot be named, the copy is decoration; rewrite until the question is obvious.
- The through-line survives shuffling. A reader who sees only three scattered slides should still get the core argument.
- Action slides stay imperative but aim the verbs at work, not at the reader's attention. "Cost one task end to end, including retries" is right. Threats dressed as advice are not.
- Keep the honest-sourcing register throughout ("reported, not confirmed"). An intellectual honesty slide is a strength, never filler.

### Rewrite pass

Go slide by slide. For each: state the one question the slide answers in plain words, then rewrite to answer it under these rules. Deliver with a veto list: the aphorisms kept, the "Not X, Y" constructions kept, and any slide where two ideas competed and one was cut.
